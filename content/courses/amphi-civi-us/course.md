## I. America before the United States {#before}

### 1. America before the English colonies {#native}

Before the arrival of Europeans, North America was already inhabited by **many different Native American peoples**. There was **no single Native American culture**: tribes had different languages, political organizations, religions and ways of life.

**Important Native American societies**

- The **Mound Builders** lived in the Ohio River Valley.
- **Cahokia** was an important Native American urban center.
- In the West, tribes such as the **Hopi** and **Zuni** developed towns and built canals.
- In the Southeast: the **Choctaw**, **Cherokee** and **Chickasaw**.
- In the Northeast, several tribes formed the **Iroquois League** (the *Great League of Peace*): the **Mohawk, Oneida, Cayuga, Seneca, Onondaga**.

:::verifier Date of Cahokia
Your notes say Cahokia dates "from the early 13th century". Reference sources place Cahokia's **peak around 1050–1200**, so it was already in decline in the 13th century. Check the date given on the lecture slides.
:::

:::complement Source : [Britannica — Cahokia Mounds](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cahokia-Mounds)
Cahokia was the largest prehistoric settlement north of Mexico, at its height roughly between 1050 and 1150.
:::

**Shared characteristics** (with great diversity, especially in religion, politics and language):

- Religious ceremonies were connected to **farming and hunting**.
- Belief in **spiritual powers in living and non-living things**.
- Land was a **common resource**: people had the right to use it, not necessarily to own it as private property.
- **Gift-exchange ceremonies**, including the **potlatch**.
- Some societies were **matrilineal** (descent traced through the mother).

### 2. How Europeans considered Native Americans {#europeans}

European attitudes were **contradictory**: Native Americans were represented either as **"noble savages"** or as **"evil creatures"**. These representations helped Europeans **justify colonization**.

| Who | Argument |
|---|---|
| **Spain and Portugal** | Native peoples had to be **converted to Christianity** |
| **Other European countries** | Native Americans **did not really own the land** |
| **Other arguments** | Native women were mistreated; Europeans brought freedom and "Christian liberty"; **mercantilism** (economic theory favoring the colonizers) |

### 3. Early settlements in the Americas {#settlements}

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **c. 1000** | The **Vikings** reach Newfoundland |
| **1492** | **Christopher Columbus** reaches the Bahamas (traditionally **October 12, 1492**) |
| **1499–1502** | **Amerigo Vespucci** explores the coast of South America — the name *America* comes from him |
| **1519** | **Hernán Cortés** conquers the Aztec Empire (capital: **Tenochtitlan**) |
| **1530s** | **Francisco Pizarro** conquers the Inca Kingdom |

European conquest was helped by **military technology** and **epidemics, especially smallpox**, which caused a dramatic decline of the Native population. The course gives:

- Mexico: approx. **20 million → 2 million** during the 16th century.
- Present-day United States: approx. **2–5 million → 250,000** by the late 19th century.
- Approx. **80 million** Native people died between 1500 and 1650.

:::attention
These are the **course's figures**. Historians' estimates of the pre-Columbian population vary a lot; quote them as "the course gives…".
:::

In the Spanish colonies, Native people were extensively used as **labor**; Spanish colonists became numerous, leading to **assimilation and miscegenation**.

### 4. The French in America {#french}

In **1534**, **Jacques Cartier** explored the region that became Canada. In the early 17th century, French **fur companies** settled in **New France**: the **fur trade** drove French expansion.

### 5. The Dutch in America {#dutch}

In **1609**, **Henry Hudson**, working for the Dutch East India Company, explored the Hudson River. The Dutch founded **New Amsterdam**, which became **New York** under English control in **1664**.

## II. From British colonies to American states {#colonies}

### 1. The 13 British colonies {#thirteen}

The British colonies developed between **1607 and 1732**, first through **private enterprises** receiving **charters from the King**.

| Date | Colony |
|---|---|
| **1585–1587** | **Roanoke** — an unsuccessful English colony |
| **1607** | **Jamestown**, founded with John Smith |
| **1620** | **Plymouth** — the Pilgrim Fathers arrive on the **Mayflower** |
| **1629** | **Massachusetts Bay** |
| **1732** | **Georgia**, the last of the 13 colonies |

From **1619**, **slavery** became part of the colonial system, with a **triangular trade** between the British colonies, the West Indies and Africa.

**The three groups of colonies**

| Group | Colonies |
|---|---|
| **New England** | Connecticut, Rhode Island, Massachusetts, New Hampshire |
| **Middle** | Delaware, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, New York |
| **Southern** | Georgia, South Carolina, North Carolina, Virginia, Maryland |

### 2. Growing tensions with Great Britain {#tensions}

At first, Britain followed a policy of **"benign neglect"** (limited control). This changed:

- **Mercantilism**: Britain wanted the colonies to serve the economic power of the Empire → **Navigation Acts**, trade restrictions, economic regulations.
- **The French and Indian Wars**, especially the **Seven Years' War (1756–1763)**: Britain won, but the war was **extremely expensive**. It ended with the **Treaty of Paris (1763)**.
- **The Proclamation of 1763**: after the **Pontiac Upheaval** (1763, also called Pontiac's War), Britain drew a **boundary** beyond which settlers could not move west. Colonists felt Britain was blocking their **westward expansion**.

### 3. Heading toward independence {#toward}

After the war, Britain needed money, so it **increased taxation and control**.

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **1765** | **Stamp Act** — tax on printed materials. Slogan: **"No taxation without representation"** (James Otis: *"Taxation without representation is tyranny"*). Colonists had **no representatives in Parliament**. |
| — | Militias and groups like the **Sons of Liberty** are created |
| **1770** | **Boston Massacre** — violence between British soldiers and colonists |
| **1773** | **Tea Act** → **Boston Tea Party** |
| **1774** | **Coercive Acts** (= **Intolerable Acts**) |

### 4. The American Revolution {#revolution}

- **First Continental Congress** in Philadelphia (1774).
- **Second Continental Congress**: May 10, 1775 → March 1, 1781. **George Washington** becomes **Commander of the Continental Army**.
- Early battles: **Lexington and Concord** (April 1775), **Bunker Hill** (June 1775), **Siege of Boston** (1775–1776).
- The **Olive Branch Petition** (July 1775) was the colonists' last attempt at peace. It failed: **George III** issued a **Proclamation of Rebellion** on **August 23, 1775**.

:::verifier Dates of the First Continental Congress
Your notes give "September 5, 1774 → May 10, 1775". Reference sources say the First Continental Congress met from **September 5 to October 26, 1774**; May 10, 1775 is the **opening of the Second** Congress.
:::

:::complement Source : [Britannica — Continental Congress](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Continental-Congress)
:::

### 5. The Declaration of Independence {#declaration}

Adopted on **July 4, 1776**. Strongly influenced by the **Enlightenment**, especially **John Locke**. It announced the colonies' **separation from Great Britain**.

Founding Fathers on the drafting committee: **Thomas Jefferson, Benjamin Franklin, John Adams, Roger Sherman, Robert Livingston**.

→ The TD studies the text itself: see « Textes fondateurs » (TD).

### 6. The end of the Revolutionary War {#end-war}

Major American victory at the **Siege of Yorktown (1781)**, Virginia, with the support of **French troops** (including the **Marquis de Lafayette**). The war officially ended with the **Treaty of Paris (1783)**: peace between Great Britain and the United States.

## III. The troubled early days of the United States {#early}

### 1. The Articles of Confederation {#articles}

After independence, the 13 colonies became **13 states**, each with its own constitution. The **Articles of Confederation** were **created in 1777, ratified in 1781, effective until 1789**.

:::def Confederation vs Federation
- **Confederation**: a union of states with a **weak** central government.
- **Federation**: a **stronger** central government with important powers above the states.
:::

**Problems of the Confederation** — Congress was very weak:

- **no president**, no national judicial system, no executive power, **only one chamber**;
- could not effectively **levy taxes**, regulate **foreign trade** or **interstate trade**;
- important decisions required a **large majority**.

### 2. Federalists vs Anti-Federalists {#federalists}

| | **Federalists** | **Anti-Federalists** |
|---|---|---|
| Figure | **Alexander Hamilton** | Thomas Jefferson (according to the notes) |
| Wanted | strong central government, common national market, common foreign policy, strong manufacturing | stronger **states' rights**, weaker federal government, **agrarian** society |
| Supporters | merchants, bankers, wealthy farmers | — |
| Fear | — | too much federal power → demand explicit protection of rights → **Bill of Rights** |

:::verifier Jefferson and the Anti-Federalists
Jefferson was in France during the ratification debates and is not usually counted among the Anti-Federalist leaders, who were mostly **Patrick Henry** and **George Mason**. He did share their demand for a bill of rights and later led the Democratic-Republicans, heirs of their positions. Keep the course's version for the exam, but be ready to nuance it.
:::

:::complement Source : [Britannica — Anti-Federalists](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Anti-Federalists)
:::

### 3. Shays' Rebellion {#shays}

In **1786**, farmers revolted (**Shays' Rebellion**). The Confederation struggled to stop it: for the Federalists, another proof of its **weakness** → growing support for a **new Constitution**.

## IV. Toward the Constitution {#toward-constitution}

**The Constitutional Convention** (the *Grand Convention*): **Philadelphia, May 25 → September 17, 1787**, presided by **George Washington**. **55 delegates** (the **Framers**) wanted a stable federal state based on the **separation of powers** (an Enlightenment idea). Two plans were proposed:

| | **Virginia Plan** | **New Jersey Plan** |
|---|---|---|
| Author | **James Madison** | **William Paterson** |
| Branches | 3 (legislative, executive, judicial) | 3 |
| Congress | **2 chambers** (House + Senate) | **1 chamber** |
| Representation | **proportional to population** | **one representative per state** (equal) |
| Supported by | **large states** | **small states** (closer to the Articles) |

:::def The Great Compromise (Sherman / Connecticut Compromise)
Congress has **two chambers**:
- **House of Representatives**: representation **proportional to population**;
- **Senate**: **two senators per state**, regardless of population.

Still in place today: **50 states → 100 senators**; **435 representatives**.
:::

## V. The Constitution {#constitution}

**Created in 1787, ratified in 1788, effective in 1789.** It is the framework of the federal government and the **supreme law** of the country. It begins with a **Preamble**: *"We the People of the United States…"* It contains **7 articles**.

| Article | Content |
|---|---|
| **I** | **Legislative** branch — **Congress** (House + Senate) |
| **II** | **Executive** branch — the **President** (first: **George Washington**, elected **1789**) |
| **III** | **Judicial** branch — federal courts, with the **Supreme Court** |
| **IV** | The **states** and their powers |
| **V** | **Amending** the Constitution |
| **VI** | **Supremacy**: "the supreme Law of the Land"; no religious test for public office |
| **VII** | **Ratification**: 9 of the 13 states needed |

:::def Checks and balances
The three branches are designed to **control each other**, to prevent one branch from becoming too powerful and to prevent **tyranny**.
:::

## VI. Congress {#congress}

| | **House of Representatives** | **Senate** |
|---|---|---|
| Members | **435** | **100** (2 per state) |
| Election | directly by citizens, according to population | directly since the **17th Amendment (1913)** |
| Term | **2 years** | **6 years** |
| Renewal | **all seats every 2 years** | **about one third every 2 years** |

## VII. How a bill becomes a law {#bill}

1. The bill is presented in the **House of Representatives**.
2. The **House votes**.
3. The **Senate** considers it: it can **accept** or **modify** it (if modified, it returns to the House).
4. The **President** can **approve** it (→ law) or **veto** it (→ back to Congress).

:::retenir
Congress can **override a presidential veto** with a **two-thirds majority in both chambers**.
**Pocket veto**: when Congress is about to end its session and the President **neither signs nor vetoes** the bill.
:::

## VIII. Powers of Congress {#powers-congress}

Congress can: establish and maintain an **army and navy**, **declare war**, **raise taxes**, **regulate interstate commerce**.

:::def The Elastic Clause
Allows Congress to make laws **"necessary and proper"** to carry out its constitutional powers. Historically supported by **Federalists** and **"loose constructionists"**; criticized by **Anti-Federalists** and **"strict constructionists"**.
:::

## IX. The President {#president}

**Requirements** (according to the notes): at least **35 years old**, U.S. resident for at least **14 years**.

:::complement Source : [U.S. Constitution, Article II, Section 1 — National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript)
The Constitution adds a third condition missing from the notes: being a **natural-born citizen**.
:::

**Indirect election**, in two stages:

1. **Popular vote**: citizens vote for a list of **electors**.
2. **Electoral vote**: the electors vote for the President.

:::def Electoral College
**538 electors**; a candidate needs **270** to win. Each state has as many electors as **House representatives + 2 senators**. **Washington, D.C. has 3 electors.**
:::

**Popular vote ≠ Electoral College vote.** A candidate can win with fewer popular votes. Examples given in the course: **1876 Rutherford B. Hayes, 1888 Benjamin Harrison, 2000 George W. Bush, 2016 Donald Trump**.

**Election calendar**: **November** citizens vote → **December** electors vote → **January 6** Congress counts the electoral votes and declares the result.

## X. Midterm elections {#midterms}

Congressional elections take place **every two years**.

| | **Presidential year** (2008, 2012, 2016, 2020, 2024, 2028) | **Midterm year** (2010, 2014, 2018, 2022, 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| President | ✅ elected | ❌ no election |
| House | all seats | all seats |
| Senate | about 1/3 | about 1/3 |

Midterms can **change the balance of power in Congress**.

## XI. Presidential powers {#powers-president}

Under **Article II**, the President is **Commander-in-Chief** of the armed forces, **makes treaties**, **appoints Supreme Court judges**. These powers are **limited** by the Constitution and the other branches. The course stresses the **increasing influence of the executive** throughout American history.

## XII. The judicial branch {#judicial}

**Article III**: the Supreme Court and federal courts. Federal judges are **appointed for life**.

:::def Judicial review
Established by **Marbury v. Madison (1803)**: the Supreme Court can **declare a law unconstitutional**. Another example of checks and balances.
:::

**Important Supreme Court cases**

- **Roe v. Wade (1973)**: laws prohibiting abortion ruled unconstitutional. **Overturned in 2022** → abortion regulation returned largely **to the states**.
- **Bush v. Gore (2000)**: dispute over the vote count in **Florida**; the Court **ordered the recount to stop**; George W. Bush won the presidency.

## XIII. States, amendments and ratification {#states}

- **Article IV — the states**: they have important responsibilities. American federalism = **federal government + state governments**.
- **Article V — amendments**: deliberately difficult. **2/3 of both Houses** approve, then **3/4 of the states** ratify.
- **Article VI — supremacy**: "the supreme Law of the Land"; no religion required for public office.
- **Article VII — ratification**: at least **9 of the 13 states**. Ratified in **1788**, **New Hampshire** being the **ninth** state.

:::def Bill of Rights
The **first ten amendments**, ratified in **1791**. Important for those who feared excessive federal power: they protect **fundamental rights and liberties**.
:::

## XIV. Slavery and the Constitution {#slavery}

Slavery was already central to American society; the Constitution contained **compromises**:

- **Three-Fifths Compromise**: enslaved people counted as **three-fifths of a person** for representation → Southern states got more representatives **without giving enslaved people political rights**.
- The **importation of enslaved people** could not be prohibited before **1808**.

These compromises show the **contradiction** between the principles of liberty of the new republic and the existence of slavery.
